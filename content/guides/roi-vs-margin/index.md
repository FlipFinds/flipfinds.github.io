**ROI compares profit with cash invested. Profit margin compares profit with revenue.** Profit is the dollar amount left after the costs you include. The same resale can have a high ROI and a much lower margin because the percentages use different denominators.

## ROI vs profit margin at a glance

| Measure | Formula | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profit | Revenue minus included costs | Dollars left from the sale |
| ROI | Profit divided by cash invested, multiplied by 100 | Profit relative to the cash committed |
| Profit margin | Profit divided by revenue, multiplied by 100 | Share of revenue left after included costs |

For the [reseller ROI calculator](/calculator/reseller-roi/), revenue is sale price plus buyer-paid shipping received. Cash invested includes purchase cost, seller shipping, packaging and other entered costs. Seller fees reduce profit but are excluded from this tool's investment denominator. These are item-level estimates; they are not your business's net margin unless all relevant expenses are accounted for.

## Work through one hypothetical sale

Suppose an item sells for $40 with no separate buyer-paid shipping. You paid $10, spend $5 shipping and $0.75 packaging, and owe a $4 seller fee. These are hypothetical costs, not a marketplace fee quote.

| Sale detail | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Revenue | $40.00 |
| Purchase cost | $10.00 |
| Seller shipping | $5.00 |
| Packaging | $0.75 |
| Seller fee | $4.00 |
| Total included costs | $19.75 |
| Profit | $20.25 |

In the public tool, cash invested is purchase cost plus seller shipping, packaging and other entered costs: $15.75 here. Seller fees are deducted from proceeds rather than included in that investment denominator. ROI is $20.25 divided by $15.75, about 128.6%.

Margin divides the same profit by revenue: $20.25 divided by $40, about 50.6%. The two percentages describe the same sale; neither is an additional amount of money.

## Does 100% ROI mean 100% profit margin?

No. With $20 invested, $40 revenue and no seller fees or other costs, profit is $20. That produces **100% ROI and 50% margin**. Adding a $4 seller fee reduces profit to $16, making ROI 80% and margin 40% under the tool's definition.

The following hypothetical comparisons use total cash invested, not purchase price alone. No buyer-paid shipping or additional costs are assumed.

| Revenue | Cash invested | Seller fees | Profit | ROI | Margin |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $40 | $20 | $0 | $20 | 100% | 50% |
| $40 | $20 | $4 | $16 | 80% | 40% |
| $100 | $50 | $10 | $40 | 80% | 40% |
| $20 | $25 | $2 | -$7 | -28% | -35% |

The middle two rows have identical percentages but different dollar profit and cash requirements. The last row shows why a loss must remain negative.

## Is ROI the same as markup?

Not necessarily. Markup commonly compares the difference between selling price and purchase cost with purchase cost. Buying for $20 and selling for $40 gives a 100% markup on purchase cost. If the sale also has a $4 fee, that does not make ROI 100%: profit is $16 and ROI is 80% before any other expenses. Shipping, supplies and other costs can widen the difference.

## Compare records with consistent definitions

A spreadsheet that includes fees in its investment denominator will show a different ROI. That is a definition difference, not necessarily a calculation error. State the denominator before comparing results from different systems.

Buyer-paid shipping adds revenue and may add selling fees. Marketplace-collected tax is not your revenue. The [shared method](/resources/fee-assumptions/) explains each input.

## Handle zero and missing data

A free item can still require shipping and supplies, making investment positive. If all investment is zero, ROI is undefined. Do not replace a missing purchase cost with zero and infer an exceptional return.

A loss should remain negative. A short selling history or incomplete costs cannot establish your typical business ROI.

If revenue is zero, margin is undefined too. An unsold item's hoped-for resale value is not completed-sale revenue. Keep forecasts separate from actual results when [reconciling inventory](/guides/inventory-reconciliation/).

## Choose the comparison that answers your question

Use a dollar target when you want a specific amount left after costs. Use ROI when comparing profit with invested cash. Also consider time, uncertainty and how long stock sits; the calculator does not automatically model these.

There is no universal "good reseller ROI" in these numbers. A percentage alone cannot tell you whether the dollar profit covers your time or whether the item will sell.
